# Summary shapes: fit the class, not a template

Choose the shape from two things: what the content is, and how the exam asks about it. The shapes below are starting points. Mix them inside one unit when the content mixes: a calculation topic in a fact-heavy unit still gets worked examples.

A good shape answers one question: **what does a student need in front of them to answer this class's exam questions?**

## Information-heavy classes: exam-question style

Examples: operating systems, networks, information systems, biology, law, history.

The student's obstacle is the amount of information and how it is organized, not understanding. So organize the summary the way the exam asks.

**The IT241 Module 11 shape**, which the student approved: one section per topic, in teaching order. Inside each topic the blocks always come in the same order:

1. **Key terms:** the term in bold first, its meaning on the next line. Never the definition first ("weird, the concept is above the thing").
2. **Rules:** a short question, then the direct answer, with the words to say highlighted. These are the multiple-choice facts.
3. **Written questions:** the exam's own forms (define, list, compare, differentiate, explain briefly), each with a short model answer as bullets: memorizable, and complete enough for full marks.
4. **Calculations:** the exam's calculation types, worked step by step with the final answer marked.
5. **Traps:** the pairs students confuse, asked as questions ("Which problem belongs to the hash table: linear search time or collisions?").

★ marks what a past final asked. Use only the exam's formats: IT241 finals are multiple choice and written, so the summary has no true/false or yes/no questions.

## Math: worked examples and methods

Examples: linear algebra, calculus, discrete math.

- For each method: **when to use it** (the cue in the question), the **steps** as a numbered list, then **one worked example**. Show every step and keep the numbers small.
- Then the **common mistakes**, and a **quick check** for the answer (for example, multiply back).
- Theorems: the statement, what it lets you conclude, and a one-line example.
- Match the exam's question length. If exam questions are short, use short examples; skip assignment-length problems.

## Physics and engineering: rules and equations

- For each equation:
  - its symbols, with units;
  - **when it applies** and when it doesn't;
  - sign conventions;
  - **one standard problem** worked through.
- Mark which equations the exam's formula sheet gives and which must be memorized.
- Conceptual rules ("the force is largest at 90°", "no force on a charge at rest") get their own short block, because multiple-choice questions test them.
- Pictures: field lines, vectors, circuits. Physics summaries need them most.

## Statistics: a decision guide

- **Which method, when:** the cue words in a question that point to each test or formula.
- **Steps** for each method, with the calculator keys if the exam allows one.
- **Interpretation sentences** in the exact form the exam expects ("reject H0; there is enough evidence that…").
- True/false belongs here only if the exam has it.

## Writing and language classes: models and templates

- For each document type: its **parts, in order**, then a short **model**, then do and don't pairs.
- Rules (grammar, style, format) as short lines, each with an example.
- Practice in the exam's form: rewrite this, identify the error, choose the better version.

## Programming classes

- Each concept: what it does, the syntax, a minimal runnable example, and its output.
- Tracing questions: code, then a step table of the variables (a real table is right here).
- Common errors and what the compiler or runtime says.

## Choosing when it isn't obvious

- Build one topic in two shapes and let the student pick.
- Ask what blocks them in this class:
  - **Understanding:** lead with explanation and examples.
  - **Amount and organization:** use exam-question style.
  - **Applying:** lead with worked examples.
